01

Problem & constraints

A collaborative robot runs the same pick-and-place cycle over and over, and some cycles go wrong: a collision, a lost can, extra joint friction, a motor commutation fault.

Labelled faults are rare in practice, so detection has to be learned from normal cycles alone; and an operator needs to know which fault happened, not just that something did.

Can physically meaningful residuals flag faulty cycles from normal data alone, at a false-alarm rate an operator would tolerate?

Constraints

  • Alarm limits must be set without touching test data.
  • No session confounders: each fault variant was recorded in its own session.
  • CPU-only and interpretable: pandas and scikit-learn.

02

My contribution

Design, implementation and evaluation

  • Engineered physics residuals (tracking errors, measured − model torque) summarised per program phase: 27 channels × 14 phases × 3 statistics.
  • Built the PCA/SPE detector and found that in-sample alarm limits are badly optimistic; replaced them with cross-validated limits.
  • Excluded supply voltage as a session confounder by design.
  • Trained the fault classifier and measured how it generalises to unseen fault variants.

Method & key decisions

Residuals a healthy servo keeps small

For each axis: target − measured position and velocity, and measured − model-computed motor torque, plus motor current. Faults happen at specific moments (grasp, transport, place), so statistics are computed per program phase.

Detector trained on normal cycles only

Normal cycles lie near a low-dimensional subspace. PCA keeps 95% of normal variance (332 components); the squared prediction error (SPE) measures what normal behaviour cannot explain.

Cross-validated alarm limits

With 332 components fitted to 948 cycles, PCA reconstructs its own training cycles almost perfectly. The in-sample 99th-percentile limit (43.8) would flag 100% of test normals. Scoring each training cycle with a PCA fitted on the other 4/5 gives a limit of 185.5 and a 1.7% false-alarm rate.

Supply voltage excluded for a physical reason

Voltage reflects the plant's supply, which differs per recording session. A model using it could learn the session instead of the fault, so it is not even loaded.

Failures, limits & next steps

✕Detection quality depends on the fault

Entangled cycles are flagged only 20% of the time and axis friction 38%. Their effect on the cycle is small, and the per-phase summary statistics dilute it.

✕Collisions get confused with one another

Foam, cable and carton collisions share a signature, and Entangled is always read as Axis friction in the confusion matrix.

✕Generalising to a new variant is hard

Holding whole fault variants out drops macro-F1 from 0.762 to 0.417: the classifier recognises known variants far better than new ones.

Limitations

  • One dataset, one robot, one task.
  • Uses the program-phase signal, which a deployment would need from the controller.
  • Test normals were recorded after training; false alarms cluster at the end of that recording, suggesting session drift.
  • MVT-Flow comparison is indicative only: this pipeline uses phase alignment, and the paper's figure is a 9-run mean of a deep model.

Next steps · what would prove it

  • Online re-calibration against session drift, evaluated on timestamped streams.
  • Event-level features (peak timing) for subtle faults such as Entangled.
  • Run the detector on a live controller stream to measure latency and alarm stability.
